Types of childcare

Moving to a new country with a child is a big step — new language, new customs, new forms. Suddenly you're not just looking for childcare, you're decoding the Dutch system somewhere between toeslagen and a waiting list. The good news: it's well organised. The less romantic news: at first, it can feel confusing. Here's the map.

Daycare center

KDV · 0–4 yrs

Regular daytime childcare for children from 0 to 4. Children attend while parents work, study, or simply need to function like a human being — yes, that counts too. For many expat families, KDV is the first real contact with the Dutch childcare and early-education system.

After-school care

BSO · 4–12 yrs

Care for school-age children from 4 to 12 who already attend primary school. BSO usually takes place after school, and sometimes during school holidays or study days too.

In-home daycare

Gastouder

A smaller, home-based form of care, often in the home of a registered childminder. It can feel more personal and calm — just be sure to check registration, safety, communication, costs and how the daily routine is organised.

What is KDV?

KDV is a daycare center for children from birth until age 4. Children spend the day in a safe environment with a daily rhythm — meals, rest, play and contact with other children. For expat kids, it's often one of the first places they naturally start hearing and understanding Dutch. A good daycare isn't just babysitting: it supports development, social skills, language, motor skills, independence and confidence in a group.

Why daycare helps expat families

Social skills

In a group, children learn to play together, wait, share and handle small conflicts. For a toddler, sharing a plastic shovel can be a full diplomatic crisis.

Dutch, naturally

Young children learn language through everyday life: dressing, eating, playing, singing, going outside. No grammar book — just repetition, play and normal routine.

Structure for parents

Many expat parents have no grandparents or neighbours nearby. Daycare brings structure and makes it easier to combine work, study, appointments and parenting.

Meeting other parents

Daycare becomes a natural place to meet other families. Sometimes it's a friendship, sometimes just a chat at the door — both help when building a new life here.

Costs & childcare allowance

Childcare in the Netherlands can be expensive — good to know before the first invoice arrives and your soul briefly leaves your body. The government offers support through kinderopvangtoeslag (childcare allowance), which you apply for through the Dutch Tax Authority (Belastingdienst).

The amount depends on
Your income
The number of childcare hours
The type of childcare
The hourly rate
Your family situation
What you usually need
The provider's LRK number
The hourly rate & number of hours
Your childcare contract
Your estimated annual income
Your partner's details, if applicable
Parents are responsible for applying and for updating any changes themselves. The Dutch system is helpful, but it expects you to keep things current — it's not exactly the "I'll just wing it" department.
3
MONTHS
Important · Don't miss this
Apply on time. In general, childcare allowance must be requested within 3 months after childcare starts — otherwise you may lose part of the allowance.

How to choose a daycare

Choosing daycare isn't only about the closest building — although, after cycling through Dutch rain with a child in the seat, distance suddenly becomes a very emotional topic. Look at three things.

1

First feeling

Do I feel welcome here? Do they notice my child?
Do they speak to children naturally and kindly?
Does the environment feel calm, clean and safe?
Do I feel comfortable asking questions?
2

Staff

How many staff are in the group? Are there familiar, regular faces?
Does each child have a mentor?
How do they follow a child's development?
How do they handle crying, separation anxiety, tiredness or conflicts?
3

Daily routine

When do children eat and sleep? Do they go outside every day?
How do they handle individual sleep rhythms?
How do they deal with allergies or special diets?

More things to check

The group & environment

Children don't need a Pinterest-perfect classroom. They need a safe place to move, explore, and occasionally decide a cardboard box is the greatest invention in human history.

Are toys within reach? Is the space safe?
Is there a quiet area?
Can children move, climb, build & explore?
Does the room match the children's age?
Communication with parents

With young children, details matter. "Everything was fine" is nice, but parents usually need more.

How will I hear about my child's day?
Will I know how they ate, slept & played?
Do they use an app or send photos?
Do they call in urgent situations?
Costs & what's included

Every daycare differs. Some include almost everything; others leave you packing half your house into one small bag. Ask about:

Meals, snacks & diapers
Formula or bottles · sleeping bag
Activities, trips & parent app
Extra days or switching days

How to prepare your child

Starting daycare is a big change for both child and parent — and yes, sometimes it's harder for the parent. The child may be stacking blocks after two days, while the parent is at home checking the app every twelve seconds.

1

Practise bottle feeding, if needed

If your baby is breastfed and will get expressed milk or formula from a bottle at daycare, start before day one.

Try the bottle in a calm environment
Let someone other than the main caregiver offer it
Use a small cloth or soft toy that smells like the parent
Practise regularly, but without pressure
Some babies accept the bottle quickly. Others look at it like you just offered them a tax form. Stay calm — it can take time.
2

Get used to sleeping in their own bed

At daycare, babies usually sleep in their own bed. A simple, repeatable sleep ritual helps the transition:

The settling-in period

A new environment means new sounds, smells, people, children and routines. At the beginning, it's completely normal if your child needs time to land.

In the first weeks, your child may…
Sleep differently
Drink less
Cry more
Seem more tired in the evening
Need more closeness at home
Give it a few weeks. Adaptation is not a race.
Prepare yourself, too

Settling in isn't only for children — parents also need time to trust a new place.

Ask questions
Agree on clear communication
Know when & how they'll contact you
Expect some emotions
Avoid blaming yourself if it feels hard
There's no need to panic after day one if your child doesn't come home looking like they've fully accepted the Dutch childcare system and filed the paperwork.
"
Putting your child in childcare is not a failure. It's a normal part of family life — especially in a country where grandma usually doesn't live downstairs with soup and emergency babysitting powers.

In one breath
The short version

Childcare, summed up

Childcare in the Netherlands is a well-organised system, but for expat parents it can feel confusing at first. The most common type of care for children from 0 to 4 years is a daycare center, called KDV in Dutch.

When choosing, look beyond price and location. Pay attention to atmosphere, communication, staff stability, daily routine, safety and how the daycare supports your child during settling-in.

Your child may need a few weeks to adjust to the new environment, people, sounds, language and routine. This is normal — and it's also normal for parents to need time to trust a new place.

"
Childcare isn't just babysitting. For many children, it becomes the place where they learn language, social skills, independence, rhythm and confidence in their new country.
